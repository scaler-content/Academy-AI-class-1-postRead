    01

    Why AI is suddenly everywhere

    AI is about seventy years old. So why did it fill your feed only recently? Two things changed.

    The model became general-purpose

    Old AI did one task. A spam filter filtered spam and could do nothing else. Today's models write, summarise, translate, answer and code, all from one model. One engine, many uses.

    The barrier collapsed

    You do not build the model. The labs spend hundreds of millions doing that, and you call the result over the internet in a few lines of code.

    Something that needed a team and six months a few years ago is now an afternoon for one person. That collapse in cost is the whole opportunity.

    What you build across the first three classes

    One real thing: a web app that takes any URL, reads the page, and gives you a clean summary.

    ClassWhat you add
    1Your first call, in code. A key, a client, three roles.
    2The prompt. You write the system prompt that makes the summary good.
    3The whole app. Scraper, interface, safety, and a public link you can send to anyone.

    This class is the first third of the build, with the concepts you need so you are not lost in it.

    Is a chatbot with a web search button an agent? Common doubt

    Partly. It uses a tool, which is one ingredient. What it does not do is choose its own sequence of steps. One tool call that you triggered is a feature. The test is who decides what happens next.

    03

    What an LLM does, and what a token is

    The autocomplete that read everything

    Your phone predicts your next word as you text. Now give that autocomplete most of the public internet, and let it predict not one word but thousands in a row. That is an LLM: the world's most well-read autocomplete.

    This sounds reductive and it is the most useful idea in the class. It explains three things:

    • Why prompting works: you are steering the prediction.
    • Why models invent things: a plausible next word is not the same as a true one.
    • Why context matters: the prediction is conditioned entirely on what is in front of it.

    Tokens are the unit it reads

    The model does not read words. It reads tokens, chunks of text that average around four characters in English. Write "I love programming" and you see three words. The model sees more, because the long word gets chopped into pieces of its own.

    Tokens matter for one very practical reason: tokens are the billing unit and tokens are the limit. You will see this on your own API bill.

    Why tokens, and not words or letters

    The model needs a fixed vocabulary, a list of things it knows. Both obvious choices fail.

    One per word

    Millions of entries across every language, plus names, slang and typos. Breaks the first time it meets a word it has never seen.

    One per letter

    Tiny vocabulary but enormous sequences: slow and expensive, and the model must rebuild spelling every time.

    Sub-word tokens

    Around a hundred thousand common chunks. Frequent words stay whole; rare ones are assembled from pieces. One vocabulary handles every language, including words invented tomorrow.

    This is a simplified splitter that shows the pattern: prose stays chunky, code fragments into brackets and underscores, and non-Latin scripts shatter into many small pieces. Counts from a real tokenizer will differ. Try one at platform.openai.com/tokenizer.

    Plain English

    Around four characters per token. The cheapest per unit of meaning.

    Code

    Tokens per character jump, because indentation, brackets and underscores each become their own token. Code costs more per line than prose.

    Hindi / non-Latin

    The count rises sharply for the same meaning. If you build for an Indian user base, this shows up on the bill.

    If I write shorter prompts, do I always pay less? Common doubt

    Less on input, yes. Output tokens usually cost two to four times more than input tokens, so a vague short prompt that triggers a long rambling answer can cost more than a precise longer one. You pay for both halves.

    04

    The trick that made all of this possible

    How do you teach a machine language without an army of people labelling billions of examples?

    The old way

    Humans label data by hand. This email is spam. This review is positive. Accurate, but you need millions of labelled examples, so it is slow and expensive.

    The shortcut

    Take any sentence from the web. Hide a word. Ask the model to guess it. The answer is already in the text, so no human labels anything. The internet becomes its own teacher: trillions of free practice questions.

    Try it · Fill the blankSelf-supervision in one sentence

    The barista handed me a hot cup of     

    Make your guess before you reveal it.

    To guess "coffee", you had to quietly know:

    • grammar
    • what a barista is
    • what is served hot
    • what fits in a cup
    • what usually follows that phrasing

    None of that was taught directly. It came out of getting very good at filling in blanks.

    Important

    Understanding emerges as a side effect of predicting text well. It also explains the failure mode: a model trained to produce plausible text will produce plausible text when it does not know. People call this hallucination, and Class 3 is largely about designing around it.

    05

    No memory, and the context window

    Between calls, the model remembers nothing. Every chat application you have used creates the illusion of memory by re-sending the conversation every single time. That has a limit, and the limit is the context window.

    Picture a desk. Everything the model can see has to fit on it: your instructions, the conversation so far, anything you pasted, and the answer it is about to write. A small desk holds a few pages. A large one holds a codebase. When you run out, papers fall off the edge, and the model genuinely loses what was at the start.

    Visualizer · The deskIllustrative token sizes

    Space is always reserved for the answer the model is about to write. Dashed, struck-through items no longer fit: the model cannot see them, which is why a long chat can start contradicting something it said twenty messages earlier.

    Important

    A large window is not the same as using it well. Accuracy drops in the middle of a very long context on every model. If something matters, put it near the start or the end, not buried in the middle of a long document.

    Context-window visualizer used in class: context-windows.damien-henry.com. This is also why today's app sends the page text on every single call rather than once, and why retrieval exists.

    06

    The dials: temperature, top-p, top-k

    The model scores every possible next token. These three settings decide how it picks from that list.

    Temperature

    How willing it is to pick something other than the most likely token. At 0 it takes the top candidate nearly every time, so the same input gives the same output. At 1 it reaches further down the list: variety, and occasionally nonsense.

    Top-k

    Caps the candidate list by count. Top-k of 5 means only the five most likely tokens are considered.

    Top-p

    Caps it by probability mass. Top-p of 0.9 keeps the smallest set of tokens whose probabilities add up to 0.9: maybe two tokens on a confident prediction, forty on an uncertain one.

    The difference worth remembering

    Top-p adapts to how sure the model is. Top-k does not.

    Visualizer · Probability dialsNext token after: "The barista handed me a hot cup of …"
    Look closely

    Scores here are illustrative, chosen to show the behaviour. Hatched bars are tokens removed from consideration. Real tool: temperature-topk-visualizer.

    • Drag temperature from 0 to 1: the bars flatten. More candidates become plausible, so output varies between runs.
    • Set top-k to 1, then drag temperature: nothing changes. Top-k of 1 overrides temperature entirely, because there is only ever one candidate.
    A real engineering choice

    A support bot wants about 0.2 so answers are consistent. A brainstorming tool wants about 1.0 because variety is the point. Today's summariser wants to be low.

    07

    The landscape: four families, and the swappable brain

    Specific model names go stale in weeks. Any list of "the best model" you memorise today will be wrong soon. So learn the structure, which does not change, and where to look up today's names.

    The four families

    Explorer · Model familiesSelect a family

    Fast models and reasoning models

    This is the most important split in the landscape right now. A fast model answers in one pass: it reads your prompt and starts writing immediately. A reasoning model spends output tokens on itself first, working through the problem before it answers. More tokens, more seconds, better results on anything with several dependent steps.

    Compare · Fast vs reasoningToggle

    The split used to be a choice between two different models. On newer open-weight models it is increasingly a dial on one model: you send a reasoning effort of low, medium or high and pay for the thinking you asked for.

    The model you use today, gpt-oss, works exactly that way. Summarising a page has no dependent steps, so you keep the effort low and it behaves like a fast model. That is matching the thinking to the job, not a budget compromise. You set that dial in Class 2; today's call stays minimal on purpose.

    The brain is swappable

    Changing which company's model your app runs on is a two-line change. Same library, same code, same message format. You point the client at a different address and name a different model.

    Why it matters

    That is the real reason to learn the structure rather than the names. You are not marrying a vendor. If a model gets better, cheaper, or is retired, you move, and your app does not care. (You will see this proved in Section 10.)

    How to read a leaderboard

    SourceThe question it answersWatch out for

    Important

    Benchmarks get gamed and contaminated. Treat a leaderboard as a shortlist, not a verdict. The real test is your own task on two or three candidates.

    What people actually build

    Foundation models and more senses

    Modern models are not only text. The same idea extended to images, audio and video gives you a foundation model: one general-purpose engine you adapt to many jobs. Show it a photo and ask a question about it. Speak to it and it speaks back.

    The eight things people build

    Almost every AI product you have seen is one of these. Select a card to see an example.

    Important

    Underneath, they are all the same shape: connect to a model, give it the right context, get a useful answer, wrap it in an interface. Learn that once and all eight become buildable. The project you start today is the sixth one: information aggregation.

    09

    Setup: your key and your .env file

    An API key is a password that lets your code use somebody's model. This class uses Groq, which gives one away free, with no billing details, and serves open-weight models very fast.

    Step 1. Get a key

    Go to console.groq.com and sign in. Open API Keys and create a key. Copy it immediately, because the site shows it once.

    Step 2. Create your .env file

    Make a folder for the project. Inside it, create a file named exactly .env, with the leading dot, and put your key in it. The file holds your secret so it never appears in the code you share. That is the only thing it does.

    .env
    # This is not Python. It is a plain text file named .env
# Keep it private. Add it to .gitignore so it never reaches GitHub.

GROQ_API_KEY=gsk_your_key_goes_here

    Step 3. Install the packages

    terminal
    # Run once in your editor's terminal. In a notebook, keep the leading ! so it runs as a shell command.
pip install openai python-dotenv
    Never share your key

    Never paste a key into code you share, and never commit one to GitHub. Bots scan public repositories for keys within minutes, and a leaked key spends real money. The key lives in .env, and .env goes in .gitignore. This one habit genuinely saves people.

    Why is my .env file invisible in the folder? Common doubt

    Files starting with a dot are hidden by default on Mac and Linux. Your editor shows it. In Finder, press Command, Shift and full stop to reveal hidden files.

    I get a "No GROQ_API_KEY found" error Troubleshooting

    The two most common causes produce the same confusing error: a key pasted with a trailing space, or the file saved as .env.txt. Check, in this order:

    1. The file is named exactly .env, not .env.txt.
    2. It sits in the same folder as your Python file.
    3. The line reads GROQ_API_KEY=gsk_... with no spaces around the =.
    10

    Your first call: six lines and three roles

    The code does four things, in order:

    1. Loads your key from .env.
    2. Creates a client, the object that knows how to talk to the model.
    3. Sends a list of messages.
    4. Prints the reply out of the response.

    That is the whole shape of every AI application you will ever write, including the one in Class 3.

    first_call.py
    # Loads the key, creates a client, sends messages, prints the reply.

import os
from dotenv import load_dotenv
from openai import OpenAI

load_dotenv()                    # reads .env so the key is available

key = os.getenv("GROQ_API_KEY")
if not key:
    raise SystemExit(
        "No GROQ_API_KEY found.\n"
        "Check three things, in this order:\n"
        "  1. The file is named exactly .env, not .env.txt\n"
        "  2. It sits in the same folder as this file\n"
        "  3. The line reads GROQ_API_KEY=gsk_... with no spaces around the ="
    )

# The same library talks to Groq. Only these two lines name a provider.
client = OpenAI(
    api_key=key,
    base_url="https://api.groq.com/openai/v1",
)

response = client.chat.completions.create(
    model="openai/gpt-oss-120b",
    temperature=0.2,             # low, because we want consistency
    messages=[
        {"role": "system", "content": "You are a witty travel guide."},
        {"role": "user",   "content": "Suggest one thing to do in Bangalore."},
    ],
)

print(response.choices[0].message.content)

    The three roles

    The messages list is the entire grammar of chat models. There are only three roles.

    RoleWho it isWhat it does
    systemYou, the builderSets the personality and the rules. Applies throughout.
    userThe person using your appThe actual request.
    assistantThe modelIts reply. To continue a conversation, you append it and resend the whole list.

    Change only the system line, say to a pirate or a tax inspector, and re-run: same code, same model, different product. Class 2 is entirely about writing that one line well.

    Visualizer · Memory is just re-sendingWhat the model receives
    Model sees

    That is Section 5 made concrete. The model has no memory, so a conversation is you re-sending the entire list every time. Nothing else is happening.

    The swappable brain, proved

    Change two lines and the same code runs on a different company's model.

    swap provider
    # Same file, same messages, different provider.
# To use OpenAI instead of Groq, change only these two things:

client = OpenAI(
    api_key=os.getenv("OPENAI_API_KEY"),   # a different key in .env
    # no base_url line at all -- OpenAI is the library's default
)

# ...and name one of their models instead:
#     model="gpt-4o-mini"
    Important

    Nothing else moves: same library, same message list, same way of reading the reply. OpenAI needs billing credit, so stay on Groq unless you already have it.

    If the code is the same, what am I actually choosing between? Common doubt

    Price, speed, context size and character. The code is the easy part. Picking well is the skill, which is what Section 7 was for.

    11

    Wrap-up and what is next

    What you can do now

    Explain the model

    What it is doing when you press enter: predicting the next token from whatever is on its desk.

    Explain why it works

    Self-supervision, and why that same trick produces confident wrong answers.

    Reason about limits

    Tokens and context are the two hard limits, and they are also the bill.

    Pick a model deliberately

    The family for its character, fast or reasoning for the shape of the task.

    And you have made a real API call, with a key you own, from code on your machine.

    When a new model launches next month, you will not need to relearn anything. Ask the same questions: which family, how much thinking does this task need, what does it cost at my volume, and is my key still in .env where it belongs.

    1. Keep today's folder and your .env file. Class 2 builds on them.
    2. Run first_call.py once more and confirm you still get a reply.
